WHG Licensing: Audit, Discussion Points & Recommendations

Internal briefing prepared in response to the TreeAlive licensing inquiry

Prepared by: Dr Stephen Gadd, Technical Director  ·  Date: 16 June 2026  ·  Audience: WHG team & (potentially) Pitt commercialization office  ·  Status: For discussion — not legal advice

Executive summary

A genealogy startup (TreeAlive) asked which licence governs WHG's published place data, and flagged that our documentation cites CC BY-NC 4.0 while our public-data page cites CC BY 4.0. A full sweep of the codebase confirms the inconsistency is larger than reported: WHG states a licence to users in at least nine distinct places, and they do not agree. The contradictions split along audience lines — contributors are told CC BY 4.0, reusers are told CC BY-NC 4.0 — which is where the genuine legal exposure lies. Crucially, much of the data WHG provides is delivered not as file downloads but live through the Reconciliation API, where multiple aggregated sources are blended into single results — a harder case with different and more consequential licensing questions, examined in §2.

The single coherent finding: WHG's aggregated index is a mix of licences (CC BY, CC BY-NC, ODbL, public domain, and bespoke terms). It cannot honestly be covered by any single blanket licence in either direction. The legally clean position and the internally consistent position are the same: state no global licence over the aggregate; let each source carry its own licence, and attach that provenance to the data wherever it leaves the platform.

This is already being addressed. A sitewide remediation — the Citations, Licences & Credit programme — was designed and locked on 6 June 2026 and is substantially built and deployed (§5). This inquiry validates that work and tells us which remaining piece to prioritise.

2. Delivery channels: downloads vs. the Reconciliation API

WHG delivers data through two very different channels, and they carry different risks:

  • File downloads hand over a single dataset or collection. Each has a known, attachable licence — the problem here (§1) is purely consistency, and it is straightforward to fix.
  • The Reconciliation API answers queries live by blending candidate matches — names, alternate names, geolocation, type — drawn from many aggregated sources at once. This is the harder case.

The reconciliation channel raises four distinct issues:

  • Blended delivery without licence context. Each reconciliation candidate is returned with only its id, name, type, score and match flag — no per-result licence or source-rights signal. A consumer (OpenRefine, or TreeAlive calling programmatically) therefore receives results spanning many differently-licensed sources with no machine-readable way to know which is which — and so cannot comply with per-source terms even if willing.
  • Blending can manufacture a derivative. Where a record merges attributes from several sources, the output is arguably a compilation of them. If any input carries ShareAlike terms (e.g. OSM/OHM under ODbL, or a BY-NC-SA source), ShareAlike is copyleft — it can force the whole blended record under the same terms, "infecting" otherwise-permissive (even public-domain) data mixed into the same record. For an aggregator, ShareAlike is a more structural problem than NC.
  • Facts vs. database right — and a US/UK split. A single name→coordinate is fact (not copyrightable, safe to reuse). But systematic, repeated extraction of a substantial part of the index via the API can trigger UK/EU sui generis database right even where no copyright subsists — which is exactly what a genealogy app reconciling at scale does. Note the asymmetry: WHG/Pitt and TreeAlive are US-based (no database right), but several upstream contributors' rights arise under UK/EU law, so one API call can be low-risk for the consumer yet breach an upstream contributor's rights.
  • It is also WHG's strongest commercial asset. The reconciliation / linking service — the matching, ranking and cross-source crosswalk — is the value WHG genuinely creates, distinct from the underlying records. So the API both complicates the rights picture and is precisely the layer WHG can legitimately put behind a commercial licence, even where it cannot license the source records themselves.

3. The two structural problems behind the audit

3.1  Contributors are told CC BY 4.0; reusers are told CC BY-NC 4.0

These are different grants. CC BY 4.0 is irrevocable, explicitly permits commercial use, and forbids the licensor from layering additional restrictions ("no additional restrictions"). So for everything that arrived through the normal upload flow, WHG may have less right to impose NC than it currently advertises — the opposite of the intuitive worry. The genuine NC exposure comes specifically from the absorbed upstream sources (e.g. Humphrey's transcripts and other NC gazetteers we have aggregated), not from the contributor pipeline.

3.2  The DOI registrations bake NC into a permanent external record

Every minted DOI registers CC-BY-NC-4.0 in DataCite. This has the longest tail of any item in the audit because DOI metadata is externally cached and hard to walk back. It should emit each dataset's actual licence before the next batch of DOIs is minted — work already specified in the remediation (§5).

4. Points for discussion

  • WHG is simultaneously licensee and licensor. We are a licensee of upstream data (much of it NC) and a licensor to parties such as TreeAlive. We cannot grant downstream what we do not hold upstream — so the aggregate index cannot be blanket-authorised for commercial reuse.
  • What WHG can legitimately commercialise. A dual-licence model (free NC + paid commercial — cf. Edinburgh's AMI Meeting Corpus, as Palak notes) is viable, but only over what WHG owns or may sublicense: the software (already BSD-3), the reconciliation / linkage service (§2), and whole contributed gazetteers whose contributors granted commercial rights. The raw aggregated place records are mostly not ours to sell.
  • "Non-commercial" is poorly defined (Palak's point). NC famously catches uses we want — commercial academic journals, tuition-charging universities — while not reliably stopping the uses we would want to prevent. NC is a weak protective mechanism on which to build a strategy.
  • TreeAlive's "source-aware attribution" posture aligns with our direction. Sam's David Rumsey-derived template is exactly the per-source model WHG is moving toward; this is a point of genuine alignment to acknowledge in any reply.

On the University of Portsmouth / Humphrey Southall terms

Humphrey's note asserts: (a) Portsmouth holds some copyright and the files are "definitely not public domain"; (b) he is content to release them as CC BY-NC-SA; and (c) "'BY' means I should be included as a co-author on any publication making substantial use." Taking these in turn — fairly, but candidly:

Largely sound: "not public domain" is defensible, though not quite as stated. The raw facts (a place-name, a coordinate) are not copyrightable; what is protectable is the compilation and — under UK law specifically — the sui generis database right, which protects the substantial investment he describes (the OCR-scraping program and the major manual student clean-up). Because that work was done by Portsmouth staff and students, the rights holder is most likely the University, not Humphrey personally — so any commercial use needs institutional sign-off, not just his.

Over-reaching — the co-authorship claim should be challenged. As a matter of Creative Commons interpretation, the "BY" / Attribution requirement means credit — naming the creator, linking the source and licence, and noting any changes. It does not confer or require co-authorship. Attribution ≠ authorship: co-authorship is governed by authorship norms (a substantive intellectual contribution to that specific new work) and/or by contract, not by a licence's attribution clause — and even academic convention does not grant authorship merely for using a dataset, however substantially. He may legitimately negotiate co-authorship as a separate, bespoke condition of access, but it cannot be derived from "BY", and as a bespoke term it binds only WHG/Portsmouth — it cannot be passed through to downstream users the way a licence can.

Why this matters operationally: a mandatory co-authorship term is in practice more restrictive than NC — it cannot be satisfied automatically, does not scale, and WHG cannot honour it downstream (we cannot auto-co-author Humphrey onto a TreeAlive feature). And the ShareAlike element he offers is the bigger structural issue for blended delivery (§2). Both point to the same need: the platform must be able to record bespoke terms precisely and gate specific sources out of channels (downloads, reconciliation) where their terms cannot be met — capability the remediation in §5 provides.

5. Work already underway to address this

This inquiry did not catch WHG unprepared. A unified Citations, Licences & Credit design was reviewed and locked on 6 June 2026, expressly to fix "inconsistent, partly-incorrect licence handling sitewide," and is now substantially implemented. Its relevant components:

  • One licence vocabulary. A new licensing app with a curated License table (SPDX ids — CC-BY-4.0, CC-BY-NC-4.0, ODbL-1.0, ODC-By-1.0, CC0-1.0, …) carrying explicit permits-commercial, share-alike and attribution-required flags, plus a custom flag for bespoke, non-SPDX terms (exactly the Portsmouth case). One vocabulary, one renderer, reused everywhere a licence is named.
  • Truthful, per-source rights. Rights statements in downloads, DOIs and the UI now come from each object's actual licence, never a hard-coded constant. Aggregates spanning multiple sources emit a list of per-source rights, never a single collapsed or "most-restrictive" licence. WHG's own curation/aggregation licence is asserted as a clearly-distinguished overlay alongside each source's rights — never instead of it.
  • Structured credit (CRediT). Contributor roles follow the NISO CRediT taxonomy via a through-model that can attach credit to datasets, collections and authority gazetteers, with degree (lead/equal/supporting) and a corresponding-author flag. This is the proper home for bespoke acknowledgements — Aucott's clean-up management, Stoner's scraping program, and any negotiated co-authorship condition — recorded as structured, exportable metadata rather than free text.
  • Attribution follows the data out. API responses gain a namespace-keyed attribution block (source name, citation, licence, rights holder, contributors) plus the WHG overlay, via one shared helper reused across search, place-detail and downloads.
  • Capture at source. The ingestion pipeline is being upgraded so every authority gazetteer's actual upstream licence, citation and rights holder is captured at ingest and pushed into the registry — replacing the stale, incorrect global "CC-BY-NC-4.0" assumption with verified per-source terms (an explicit audit of every namespace is in progress).

Status: the foundation — licence model, per-source rights, structured CRediT credit, and the API attribution block — is built and largely in production. The remaining pieces are (i) the per-namespace authority-licence audit and ingest capture (in progress on the development branch and the indexing pipeline), and (ii) attaching per-result provenance to reconciliation responses (§6, recommendation 3) — the one place the new attribution block is not yet wired in, which this inquiry tells us to prioritise.

6. Recommendations

6.1  Single coherent position to assert everywhere

WHG's own software is BSD-3. WHG editorial content and value-add default to CC BY-NC 4.0. Contributed and absorbed datasets each carry their own licence, shown on the item page and attached to the data wherever it leaves the platform — reuse must respect the source's terms. There is no single licence over the aggregate.

6.2  For the reply to Sam / TreeAlive

  • Warm "stay tuned" is appropriate; his current free use is fine today regardless of how this resolves.
  • Be honest that the published place data is a mix; he should not assume commercial rights over the aggregate, nor over bulk reconciliation output.
  • Specific CC BY / CC0 sources are safe for any use; a future commercial path would be a negotiated licence over WHG's reconciliation layer, not the raw records.
  • Acknowledge his source-aware attribution approach as aligned with WHG's direction.

6.3  For the Pitt commercialization conversation

Frame the question precisely so the meeting is productive:

"Given a mixed corpus (CC BY, CC BY-NC, ODbL/share-alike, public domain, and bespoke terms such as required co-authorship), what can Pitt/WHG lawfully offer commercially — and how should a dual licence be structured around the WHG-owned value-add (the reconciliation/linkage service) rather than the aggregated source data?"

6.4  Capture the user's use case, and gate on it

The per-source licence flags only become enforceable once we know what the user intends to do. The recommendation is therefore to ask users to declare their use case at the point of access — and to gate resources on that declaration combined with each source's terms:

  • Where to capture it. At download (a per-download attestation, extending today's "by clicking download you agree" consent) and at API-token issuance (declared once when the token is granted, rather than on every request — the reconciliation service is already token-authenticated).
  • Default to non-commercial; offer an override. The declaration should default to non-commercial use — the safe presumption that fits the great majority of WHG's users and grants the full set under NC terms with no friction. A commercial use is then an explicit opt-in / override: the user actively declares it, which is what triggers both the gating and the commercial-enquiry funnel below. This keeps the common path frictionless while making the consequential choice deliberate and recorded.
  • How it gates. The platform combines the declaration with the per-source licence flags. The default non-commercial path receives the full set under the appropriate terms; a commercial override is served only the commercially-licensed subset (CC BY, CC0, ODC-By, …) and is excluded from NC and bespoke-restricted sources (e.g. the Portsmouth material). This declaration is the input that drives the channel gating in the table below.
  • Why it is protective. The declaration creates an attestation record evidencing WHG's good-faith enforcement of upstream NC and bespoke terms, and places compliance responsibility on the declaring user — a meaningful shield for a platform redistributing third-party data.
  • It doubles as a commercial funnel. A commercial declaration can route the user to a licensing enquiry (the Pitt-office path) rather than a flat refusal — turning the gate into lead generation. This is precisely the TreeAlive scenario.

6.5  Will NC-gating gut the commercial offering?

A reasonable objection: if we filter the API to commercially-usable sources, are the results still useful? For the great majority of queries, yes — because the commercial-incompatible material is a small, specialist minority sitting on top of a backbone that is almost entirely commercial-compatible. The four largest sources alone — roughly 29 million records — are all commercial-compatible, before counting Pleiades, LOC, Natural Earth and the rest. Three factors make the practical degradation gentler still:

  • Places are heavily duplicated across authorities. Reconciliation returns candidates; if the best match sits in an NC source, the same place usually also exists in GeoNames/Wikidata/TGN, so the commercial user still gets a match — not nothing. The real loss is the long tail of places that exist only in a restricted contributed set.
  • WHG's own value-add is unaffected. The matching, ranking and cross-source linkage — the part WHG owns and would be selling — is served regardless of which source records pass the filter.
  • The loss is concentrated, not uniform. Commercial users lose precisely the specialist niches (detailed Chinese historical units, Ottoman-era places, parish-level British transcripts) — which are also where commercial demand is thinnest. A genealogy product like TreeAlive is overwhelmingly served by the global backbone.

So the realistic risk is not "useless"; it is two deliberate design decisions:

  1. Whether to include share-alike sources in the commercial subset. OSM, OHM (ODbL) and GB1900 (CC BY-SA) are commercial-OK but copyleft. Including them (with an obligations notice) preserves coverage; excluding them keeps the subset obligation-free but is where a real dent would be felt — British historical coverage (GB1900) and detailed/modern geometry (OSM/OHM).
  2. Accepting the niche loss of the restricted contributed sets — acceptable, and arguably correct, since those are the contributors whose terms forbid commercial reuse.

What ShareAlike (copyleft) requires of the end-user

ShareAlike licences — ODbL (OSM, OHM) and CC BY-SA (GB1900) — permit commercial use but attach a copyleft obligation that can propagate into the user's own work. A commercial consumer must:

  • Attribute the source, as with any attribution licence; and
  • Re-share derivatives under the same terms. Under CC BY-SA, any adaptation of the material must itself be licensed CC BY-SA. Under ODbL, if the user builds and publicly uses a derivative database (e.g. ingests the place data into their own enhanced place-database and distributes it), that derivative database must be offered under ODbL with its alterations made available.

The practical consequence: a commercial user generally cannot keep a derived place-database proprietary if they distribute it, and SA terms can be incompatible with other licences in their stack.

A distinction that matters for app-builders: ODbL's copyleft attaches to the database, not necessarily to the end-product (its "Produced Work"). An app that merely displays OSM-derived data must attribute and keep any modified database open, but is not forced to open-source its application. CC BY-SA is broader — it reaches adaptations of the content itself, not just databases. By contrast ODC-By (TGN) and CC BY are attribution-only — no share-alike — making them the cleanest sources for a commercial consumer.

The upshot: SA sources are usable commercially but carry obligations the consumer must accept knowingly — which is exactly why attaching per-result provenance to reconciliation responses (recommendation 3) matters: the consumer needs to see which candidates are share-alike to decide whether to use and comply, or to filter them out.

Before committing to the gated commercial offering, measure two numbers: (i) record_count per namespace from the registry, and (ii) each namespace's frequency in a sample of real reconciliation responses — what matters is a source's share of actual hits, not its share of the index. The qualitative conclusion (backbone is commercial-safe; restricted material is a specialist minority) is robust regardless, but these two figures quantify the niche loss precisely.
